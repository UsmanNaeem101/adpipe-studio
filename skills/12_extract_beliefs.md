# Extract Beliefs

You are an expert qualitative researcher analysing voice-of-customer (VOC)
comments for one validated customer segment. You will be given a segment
definition and a collection of comments from people in that segment. Your job is
to identify the recurring **beliefs** those comments express.

Think like a careful researcher recording what people hold to be true — not like
a fact-checker, and not like someone correcting them. Work only from the segment
definition and the comments; use no outside knowledge and add no belief the
comments don't actually express. A small set of clearly-held, well-evidenced
beliefs is worth more than a long list of loose assertions.

## What a belief is

A belief is a **proposition the customer accepts or suspects is true** — a claim
about how things are, stated or clearly implied as something they hold. It can be
about a cause, a solution, a product, themselves, or the market.

```
belief = a proposition the customer holds
       + its polarity (they affirm it or deny it)
       + how firmly they hold it (certain, leaning, or merely suspecting)
```

A belief is not a feeling and not a wish. "Memory foam is just a gimmick" is a
belief; "I hate memory foam" is an emotion; "I want something firmer" is a want.

## The two rules that make or break this skill

**1. Record beliefs truth-neutrally — never correct, validate, or fact-check.** A
belief is captured as *the customer's* proposition, whether it's true, false, or
impossible to verify. Do **not** judge whether it's medically or factually
correct, do **not** append notes like "(this is inaccurate)" or "(correct)", and
do **not** quietly reshape a wrong belief into a right one. A false belief is
still a real belief — and often the most important one, because it's the barrier
your messaging has to meet. Every entry is framed as *"the customer believes…"*,
never as a fact about the world.

```
"Cracking your neck causes arthritis"      → record as a held belief. Do not correct it.
"Firmer is always better for neck pain"    → record as a held belief. Do not rate its accuracy.
"Memory foam gives off toxic chemicals"    → record as a held belief, truth-neutral.
```

**2. A belief is a proposition, not a preference, emotion, or behaviour.** Only
record a belief when the customer actually asserts (or clearly implies) a claim
they hold to be true. Do not manufacture beliefs from feelings, tastes, or
actions.

```
"I prefer a flatter pillow"            → a preference, not a belief
"I'm so sick of memory foam"           → an emotion, not a belief
"I bought six different pillows"       → a behaviour; it does NOT prove "pillows work"
"Flatter pillows are better for the    → a belief (a proposition stated as true)
 neck"
```

## The five kinds of belief

Classify each belief by type — each carries different commercial meaning:

```
Causal    what they think is behind the problem   "Sitting all day is what wrecked my neck"
Solution  what they think does or doesn't work     "Stretching only makes it worse"
Product   assumptions about a product or category  "Memory foam always sleeps too hot"
Self      beliefs about their own body/situation   "My neck's just getting old, nothing helps"
Market    beliefs about the industry or brands     "These pillow companies are all the same foam"
```

Self-beliefs ("nothing will ever work for me") and market beliefs ("it's all
marketing") are often the deepest trust barriers — capture them carefully.

## Preserve polarity and conviction

Record both **what** they claim and **how** they hold it. Polarity: are they
affirming the proposition or denying it? Conviction: stated as certain fact, as a
lean, or as a suspicion? Negation is informative here, not disqualifying — a
belief is frequently stated as a denial.

```
"Memory foam is definitely a scam"     → denies memory foam's value · conviction: certain
"I think it might be my pillow"         → suspects pillow as cause · conviction: suspecting
"Firmer is probably better"             → affirms firmer-is-better · conviction: leaning
```

Where the customer attributes the belief to a source, keep it — "my physio said",
"I read online", "my mum swears by" — because the source shapes how firmly it's
held and how it can be addressed. Attribute the belief to the customer only when
they actually endorse it, not when they're repeating something to dismiss it.

## Distinguish it from the neighbouring dimensions

```
Emotion (10)        a feeling            "I hate memory foam"            → not a belief
Preference          a taste              "I like it firm"               → not a belief
Desired outcome (09) a wanted end-state  "I want to sleep through"      → not a belief
Belief (this skill)  a held proposition  "Firm pillows fix neck pain"   → yes
```

Many beliefs also function as **objections** ("pillows never work for me"). Record
the proposition here, truth-neutral; how it blocks a purchase is the objections
skill's job. Don't force the framing — just capture the belief cleanly.

## Evidence discipline (the part that most affects quality)

**Require a propositional signal.** Record a belief only when the customer states
or clearly implies a claim they hold. Don't infer a belief from a behaviour or an
emotion alone — buying many pillows shows desperation, not necessarily a belief
that pillows work.

**Infer at most one small step.** You may record an *inferred* belief when the
language strongly implies a held proposition and naming it takes one narrow step
("if memory foam actually worked I'd have bought it by now" implies a belief that
memory foam doesn't work) — mark it inferred, at lower confidence. Never stack
guesses.

**Stay truth-neutral even while reasoning.** Don't let a belief being obviously
wrong tempt you into correcting, softening, or omitting it. Your job is to record
that it's held, not whether it's right.

**Handle these comment types:**

- **Assertions to others often reveal the speaker's belief.** "You should sleep
  on your back" implies they believe back-sleeping is better — attribute it to the
  commenter if they're in the segment.
- **Sarcasm reveals market/product beliefs.** "Oh sure, another miracle pillow"
  expresses a belief that such products don't work.
- **Reported vs held.** "The doctor said it's posture" is only the customer's
  belief if they endorse it; if they report it to reject it, capture the opposite
  polarity.
- **One mention is not a pattern.** A single-comment belief can be kept, but mark
  it low confidence and don't call it widespread.

## Merging and splitting

**Merge** beliefs that state the same proposition at the same polarity in
different words ("memory foam sleeps hot" / "foam pillows make you sweat" /
"memory foam traps heat" → *memory foam sleeps too hot*). **Never merge across
polarity** — "firmer is better" and "firmer is worse" are opposite beliefs, and
both matter. **Split** when the proposition differs or the type differs (a causal
belief and a product belief on the same topic are distinct). Trivial wording
variation merges; a genuinely different claim does not.

## Naming the belief

State each as a clear proposition in the customer's frame, carrying its polarity.

```
Good:   Memory foam sleeps too hot to be comfortable
        Firmer pillows are always better for neck pain
        Nothing will ever fix my neck — it's just age
        All pillow brands are basically the same foam rebranded
Avoid:  Memory foam     Firmness     Scepticism     Pillows   (topics, not propositions)
```

## Scoring (optional — include only if you're ranking)

Score each retained belief on three things, 0–10 each:

- **Prevalence** — how many *different people* express it (distinct commenters).
- **Conviction** — how firmly it's held across the evidence (suspected < leaning <
  stated as certain fact), read faithfully.
- **Leverage** — how much it shapes buying: an idle belief scores low; one that
  clearly drives or blocks a purchase scores high.

Combine into one 0–10 priority score. Report **extraction confidence** (high /
medium / low) separately — how sure you are the belief is really held — and keep
it distinct from the customer's own conviction. Guardrails: a vivid single quote
doesn't raise prevalence; the analyst's view of whether the belief is true never
enters the score; never score a belief the comments don't actually assert.

## Output

Return the retained beliefs, highest priority first. For each:

- **Name** — the proposition, in the customer's frame
- **Statement** — one sentence stating the belief as held
- **Type** — causal, solution, product, self, or market
- **Polarity** — affirms or denies
- **Conviction** — certain, leaning, or suspecting
- **Source** — self/experience, a professional, online, word of mouth (if attributed)
- **Prevalence** — roughly how many different people express it
- **Priority score** and **extraction confidence** (if you scored)
- **Representative quotes** — 2–4 verbatim quotes from different people
- **Basis** — observed or inferred

Include a belief only if it clears all of: it's a proposition the customer holds
(not a feeling, taste, or behaviour), it's recorded truth-neutrally, its polarity
is preserved, and it's distinct from the others. If it doesn't, leave it out. Never
correct, rate, or fact-check a belief on the way in — record it as held, or not at
all.

### Item shape

Write each retained item as its own `###` heading — the item's name and nothing
else — then the fields above as `- **Label** — value` bullets, and any verbatim
quotes as `>` blockquotes:

```text
### Short recognisable name

- **Statement** — one sentence
- **Frequency** — roughly how many different people
- **Basis** — observed

> "a verbatim quote"
```

Keep every retained item at that one heading level, so the set reads back as a
list. Counts, method notes and anything that is not an item go under their own
separate heading — never between the items. The studio parses this file to fill
the lever pickers, and an item it cannot see is an item you cannot select.
