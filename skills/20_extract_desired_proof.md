# Extract Desired Proof

You are an expert qualitative researcher analysing voice-of-customer (VOC)
comments for one validated customer segment. You will be given a segment
definition and a collection of comments from people in that segment. Your job is
to identify the recurring **desired proof** those comments express — the specific
evidence people need to see before they'll believe a claim, trust the brand, or
buy.

This is your evidence checklist: every proof demand is an asset your page has to
produce — a specific testimonial, a demo, a certificate, a guarantee. Work only
from the segment definition and the comments; use no outside knowledge, and record
only evidence demands that reduce a genuine doubt.

## What desired proof is

Desired proof is **an evidence demand that reduces a specific doubt** — the thing a
customer says (or clearly signals) they'd need to see before they'd believe.

```
desired proof = a demand for evidence
              + that reduces a specific doubt or uncertainty
              + about a claim, a capability, a risk, or the brand
```

Proof is defined by the doubt it answers. Evidence floating free of a doubt isn't
proof — it's a feature request or a buying criterion. Always tie the proof to what
it's meant to resolve.

Where it sits among the skills you've built:

```
Buying criterion (17)   a standard the product must MEET   "must be under £60"
Objection (18)          resistance to the purchase          "probably just marketing hype"
Desired proof (this)    the evidence that would DISSOLVE    "show me real before/afters from
                        the doubt behind the objection        people like me, not stock reviews"
```

Proof is the antidote to an objection. Where you can, link each proof demand to the
objection or risk it answers — that pairing is the whole point.

## The two rules that make or break this skill

**1. Proof must reduce a doubt — not just be a nice-to-have.** If there's no
uncertainty or disbelief behind it, it isn't desired proof. A wish for a lower
price is a buying criterion; a demand to *see it actually works before I believe
the claim* is proof.

```
"I want it under £60."                            → a buying criterion (a standard to meet)
"It should come with a nice case."                → a feature preference
"Everyone claims miracle results — show me it     → desired proof: demonstrable efficacy,
 works on someone with actual nerve pain."           tied to an effectiveness doubt
```

**2. Preserve what *kind* of proof convinces this person.** Segments differ sharply
in what they trust — some want peer reviews, some want a clinician's word, some
only believe it once they've tried it themselves, some just want a money-back
guarantee so the risk is off them. "Wants proof" is thin; "won't trust reviews,
wants to see a doctor or physio recommend it" tells you exactly which asset to
build. Keep the proof *type*.

## Stated proof and implied proof

Proof demands come **explicit** ("show me studies") and **implied** — an objection
or a sceptical question that only a specific piece of evidence would settle. "Sounds
too good to be true" implies a demonstration/before-after demand; "how do I know it
won't go flat like the last one?" implies a durability-proof demand. Capture implied
proof, marked **inferred**, at lower confidence — and always attach the doubt it's
inferred from, or you're guessing.

Note the **trust register** the person defaults to, because it predicts what will
land:

```
Social / peer         believes other buyers        → reviews, UGC, "people like me"
Authority / clinical  believes experts / science    → endorsements, studies, certifications
Experiential          believes only their own test  → trial, demo, try-at-home
Risk-reversal         doesn't need to believe, just  → guarantee, easy returns
                      needs the downside removed
```

## Distinguish it from the neighbours

The trap is **proof vs buying criterion** (17). A criterion is a standard the
product must *meet* ("must be firm enough"); proof is evidence that a *claim is
true* ("prove it stays firm"). Same attribute, different job — one is a spec, the
other is the believing. And **proof vs objection** (18): the objection is the doubt
("probably hype"); the proof is what dissolves it ("independent lab results"). Record
the doubt in 18, the evidence demand here, linked.

## Evidence discipline (the part that most affects quality)

**Require a doubt.** Record a proof demand only where there's an uncertainty or
disbelief it addresses. Don't manufacture proof needs from general enthusiasm or
from feature wishes.

**Mark implied proof needs as inferred.** When you're reading a proof demand out of
an objection or a question rather than an explicit request, say so and drop the
confidence — and name the doubt it came from.

**Don't confuse it with a buying criterion.** A standard to meet is not a demand for
evidence. If in doubt, ask: does this ask the product to *be* something, or to
*prove* something?

**Preserve the proof type and the customer's own words**, and keep single-comment
proof demands at low confidence.

## Merging and splitting

**Merge** proof demands of the same type answering the same doubt ("show me it
works" / "any real evidence it helps" / "prove it's not just hype" → *demonstrable
efficacy for the core claim*). **Split** when the type differs or the doubt differs —
a demand for durability proof and a demand for efficacy proof are distinct even
though both are scepticism. Preserve the doubt-link through any merge.

## Naming the proof demand

State it as the evidence wanted, carrying the doubt it settles.

```
Good:   Wants real before/afters from people with the same problem
        Won't trust reviews — wants a clinician's endorsement
        Needs a money-back guarantee to take the risk off
        Wants proof it stays supportive past a few months
Avoid:  Proof     Reviews     Trust     Evidence   (bare labels — no form, no doubt)
```

## Commercial implication (honest)

Each proof demand maps to a specific asset the page must carry — a UGC clip, a
before/after set, a certification badge, a named guarantee, a comparison table.
Prioritise proof that answers the highest-blocking objections. Two honesty
guardrails: only offer proof you can actually substantiate — a demanded clinical
study you don't have is a *gap to note*, not something to fabricate or imply; and
for a health-adjacent product, efficacy and clinical proof are exactly where
overclaiming creates compliance risk, so match the proof you show to what you can
defend. Building the asset is a downstream job.

## Scoring (optional — include only if you're ranking)

Score each retained proof demand 0–10 each on:

- **Prevalence** — how many *different people* demand it.
- **Doubt strength** — how blocking the doubt behind it is (mild curiosity low; a
  stated dealbreaker doubt high).
- **Evidence strength** — how clearly the demand (and its doubt) is stated vs
  inferred.

Combine into a 0–10 priority. Report **extraction confidence** separately.
Guardrails: a feature wish with no doubt never scores; a vivid single quote doesn't
raise prevalence; never invent a doubt to justify a proof.

## Output

Return the retained proof demands, highest priority first. For each:

- **Name** — the evidence wanted, carrying its doubt
- **Statement** — one sentence: what they need to see and why
- **Type** — demonstration, before-and-after, reviews/testimonials, expert endorsement, clinical/scientific, guarantee/trial, materials/specs, comparison, durability, or return policy
- **Doubt addressed** — the objection, uncertainty, or risk it dissolves
- **Trust register** — social/peer, authority/clinical, experiential, or risk-reversal
- **Prevalence** — roughly how many different people demand it
- **Priority score** and **extraction confidence** (if you scored)
- **Representative quotes** — 2–4 verbatim quotes from different people
- **Related objection / risk** — the concept it answers, linked
- **Basis** — stated or implied
- **Asset implication** — one honest line: the specific asset it calls for

Include a proof demand only if it clears all of: it reduces a genuine doubt (not a
feature wish), its proof type is preserved, and — if inferred — the doubt it came
from is named. If it doesn't, leave it out.

### Item shape

Write each retained item as its own `###` heading — the item's name and nothing
else — then the fields above as `- **Label** — value` bullets, and any verbatim
quotes as `>` blockquotes:

```text
### Short recognisable name

- **Statement** — one sentence
- **Frequency** — roughly how many different people
- **Basis** — observed

> "a verbatim quote"
```

Keep every retained item at that one heading level, so the set reads back as a
list. Counts, method notes and anything that is not an item go under their own
separate heading — never between the items. The studio parses this file to fill
the lever pickers, and an item it cannot see is an item you cannot select.
