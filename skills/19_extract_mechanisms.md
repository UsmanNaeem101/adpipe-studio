# Extract Mechanisms

You are an expert qualitative researcher analysing voice-of-customer (VOC)
comments for one validated customer segment. You will be given a segment
definition and a collection of comments from people in that segment. Your job is
to identify the recurring **mechanisms** those comments express — the causal
explanations people give for *why the problem happens* or *why a solution works
or fails*.

This is your causal map: it's what lets copy speak to the customer's own theory
of their problem in their own words, and position the product's mechanism as the
fix. Work only from the segment definition and the comments; use no outside
knowledge, and record only genuine causal claims — not descriptions, not pains,
not praise.

## What a mechanism is

A mechanism is **a causal claim: a cause linked to an effect, via an
explanation** — the "how" or "why" in the middle. It's not the pain, and it's not
a bare description. It's the *because*.

```
mechanism = a causal claim
          + a cause linked to an effect
          + via an explanation (the "how" — the intermediary step)
          + about the problem (why the pain happens)
            or the solution (why a fix works, or why it failed)
```

Two types, extracted separately:

- **Problem mechanism** — why the pain occurs. *"It's too flat, so my head drops
  and my neck bends sideways all night — that's why it aches."*
- **Solution mechanism** — why a fix works or fails (a *failure mechanism* is just
  a solution mechanism pointed at a past attempt). *"The contour fills the gap
  under my neck, so my spine stays neutral."* / *"The foam went flat, so the
  support quietly disappeared."*

Where it sits among the skills you've built:

```
Pain / problem (0x)      the effect or state             "I wake up with a stiff neck"
Belief (12)              a held proposition              "memory foam sleeps hot"
Failed solution (14)     the fact an attempt failed      "the last pillow went flat in a week"
Objection (18)           resistance to THIS purchase     "why would yours be any different"
Mechanism (this skill)   the causal claim explaining     "it went flat, so the support vanished
                         WHY — cause → via → effect        and my head sank — that's why the
                                                           stiffness came back"
```

A mechanism frequently *draws on* a belief or explains a failed solution — but
what makes it a mechanism is the causal link. Link it to the concept it draws on
where you can.

## The two rules that make or break this skill

**1. A mechanism is a causal claim — a description or a pain is not one.** The
test is simple: is there a *because* / a *so* / a *how*? Venting the pain,
describing the product, or praising an outcome is not a mechanism unless it
carries the causal link.

```
"My neck kills every morning."                   → a pain, not a mechanism (effect only)
"This pillow is too soft."                        → a description, no causal claim
"It's too soft, so I sink in and my head tips     → a mechanism: soft → sink → head tips back
 back — that's what wrecks my neck."                 → nerve/neck pain
```

**2. Preserve the causal chain — cause → via → effect — and flag it when it's
incomplete.** The value is in the *via*, the "how." "Loft causes pain" is thin;
"low loft lets the head drop below the shoulder line, bending the neck sideways,
which is why it aches" carries the chain. When a commenter gives cause→effect with
no via ("flat pillows just wreck my neck"), record it and mark the chain
incomplete — don't invent the middle, and don't discard it.

## The mechanism domains

Classify each by the *kind* of cause it invokes:

```
Biomechanical /   posture, alignment, loft,        "my head drops and the neck cranes up"
 postural          head-drop, spine angle
Material /        firmness, density, going flat,   "it sinks like a marshmallow / went flat"
 structural        edge collapse
Thermal           heat retention, sweating,        "the foam traps heat so I overheat and wake"
                   night-waking to cool down
Physiological     nerve compression, muscle        "it pinches the nerve / my jaw stays tense"
                   tension, circulation, TMJ
Behavioural       position changes, flipping or    "I fold it in half all night to get height"
                   folding the pillow, adjusting
```

## Stated mechanisms, partial mechanisms, and conviction

Mechanisms come **explicit** ("it's flat, so my head drops — that's why it hurts")
and **partial** — the causal link is gestured at but the via is left unsaid
("cheap pillows and my neck do NOT get along" implies a material→pain mechanism
without spelling out the how). Capture partial ones marked as **implied**, at
lower confidence, with the chain flagged incomplete — one narrow step, never the
invented middle.

Also note how firmly the mechanism is **held** — because a theory the customer
states as settled fact is a stronger copy foundation than one they float:

```
Asserted as fact   "this is EXACTLY why it happens"     — settled, load-bearing
Offered as theory  "I reckon it's because..."           — held but hedged
Tentative guess    "maybe it's the foam? not sure"      — floated, low weight
```

## Distinguish it from the neighbours

A **belief** (12) becomes a mechanism only when it carries a causal *because*:
"memory foam sleeps hot" is a belief; "memory foam sleeps hot *because* it's dense
and traps body heat, *so* I overheat and wake" is a mechanism. A **pain** is the
effect the mechanism explains, not the mechanism itself. A **failed solution**
(14) is the historical fact an attempt failed; the mechanism is *why* it failed —
record the fact in 14 and the causal explanation here, linked. When a comment
gives both a belief and its causal justification, the causal part is the
mechanism.

## Evidence discipline (the part that most affects quality)

**Require the causal claim.** Record a mechanism only where a cause is linked to
an effect. Don't manufacture mechanisms from bare descriptions, pains, or
praise/complaints with no "why."

**Don't infer the via from the outcome alone.** If someone says a pillow "ruined
my neck" with no explanation, you have a pain and a failed solution — not a
mechanism. Don't supply the causal middle they didn't state.

**Don't validate or correct it.** Record the mechanism as the customer holds it,
even where it's physiologically wrong ("it realigns the nerves"). Whether it's
*true* is a separate axis (validation status, below); correcting it is not this
skill's job.

**Keep provenance and validation on separate axes.** *Who* stated it (customer /
practitioner / brand / inferred) is not *how well-supported* it is. A brand can
state a bogus mechanism; a customer can state a sound one. Never upgrade a
mechanism's credibility just because a physio or the brand said it.

**Preserve the customer's own words** for the causal claim — the verbatim is the
copy asset. And keep single-comment mechanisms at low confidence — don't call them
widespread.

## Merging and splitting

**Merge** mechanisms that make the *same causal claim*, however differently worded
("too soft" / "no support" / "I just sink in" / "like sleeping on a marshmallow"
→ *the pillow is too soft, so the head sinks and the neck loses support*). Merge on
the **via**, not the wording.

**Split — and this is the trap — when the causal claim differs, even if the effect
is identical.** "It's too high, so my neck cranes up" and "it's too soft, so my
head sinks down" both end in morning neck pain, but they are *opposite*
mechanisms. Never merge on a shared outcome. Preserve the chain through any merge.

## Competing mechanisms

When several distinct mechanisms explain the *same* pain, don't adjudicate —
cluster them and record the distribution (how many *different people* back each).
The distribution **is** the finding: it tells copy which explanation to lead with,
because you build the ad around the mechanism the customer already believes.

```
Same pain — "morning neck stiffness" — three competing mechanisms:
  loft too low      →  head drops, neck bends           (11 people)
  too soft          →  head sinks, support lost         (7 people)
  runs hot          →  tossing/repositioning all night  (3 people)
```

Link competing mechanisms to each other; note any direct contradiction a commenter
makes ("everyone blames the height — for me it was the firmness").

## Naming the mechanism

State it as a causal statement carrying the chain, in the customer's framing.

```
Good:   Low loft lets the head drop, cranking the neck sideways all night
        Foam goes flat within weeks, so the support quietly disappears
        Sinks in too deep, so the head tips back and pinches the nerve
        Traps body heat, so they overheat and wake to flip the pillow
Avoid:  Loft     Firmness     Heat     Alignment   (bare labels — no cause, no via, no effect)
```

## Commercial implication (honest)

For each, you may note in one line what the mechanism opens up for copy — the true
angle it calls for (e.g. *"foam goes flat" → lead with support-retention/durability
proof, not just first-night comfort*). Two honesty guardrails:

- Match your product's *real* mechanism to the believed one. Don't claim a
  counter-mechanism the product doesn't actually deliver.
- Where the customer's believed mechanism is physiologically shaky, you can speak
  to the *felt* experience, but don't repeat a false causal claim as your own
  medical claim — mechanisms that assert medical causation are exactly the ones
  that create health-claim compliance risk.

Writing the copy is a downstream skill's job.

## Scoring (optional — include only if you're ranking)

Score each retained mechanism on three things, 0–10 each:

- **Prevalence** — how many *different people* state it (distinct commenters).
- **Conviction** — how firmly it's held: a tentative guess scores low; a mechanism
  asserted as settled fact scores high.
- **Evidence strength** — how completely the causal chain is actually stated: a
  full cause→via→effect scores high; an inferred via scores low.

Combine into one 0–10 priority score. Report **extraction confidence** (high /
medium / low) separately. Guardrails: a vivid single quote doesn't raise
prevalence; a bare description with no causal claim never scores; never invent the
via.

## Output

Return the retained mechanisms, highest priority first. For each:

- **Name** — the mechanism as a causal statement (cause → via → effect)
- **Statement** — one sentence: the full causal claim
- **Type** — problem mechanism or solution mechanism (note if it's a failure mechanism)
- **Domain** — biomechanical/postural, material/structural, thermal, physiological, or behavioural
- **Causal chain** — cause · via · effect (flag if the via is missing → chain incomplete)
- **Source type** — customer, practitioner, brand, or inferred
- **Validation status** — folk-belief, plausible, practitioner-asserted, externally-supported, or contradicted (recorded, *not* treated as truth for copy)
- **Conviction** — asserted-as-fact, theory, or tentative
- **Prevalence** — roughly how many different people raise it
- **Priority score** and **extraction confidence** (if you scored)
- **Representative quotes** — 2–4 verbatim quotes from different people
- **Competing mechanisms** — other mechanisms for the same effect, linked
- **Related belief / failed solution** — the concept it draws on, if known
- **Basis** — stated or implied (and chain complete or partial)
- **Copy implication** — one honest line

Include a mechanism only if it clears all of: it makes a genuine causal claim (not
a bare description or a pain), its causal chain is preserved (or the gap flagged),
and it's recorded as the customer holds it — without validation or correction. If
it doesn't, leave it out — a description with no "because" is not a mechanism.

### Item shape

Write each retained item as its own `###` heading — the item's name and nothing
else — then the fields above as `- **Label** — value` bullets, and any verbatim
quotes as `>` blockquotes:

```text
### Short recognisable name

- **Statement** — one sentence
- **Frequency** — roughly how many different people
- **Basis** — observed

> "a verbatim quote"
```

Keep every retained item at that one heading level, so the set reads back as a
list. Counts, method notes and anything that is not an item go under their own
separate heading — never between the items. The studio parses this file to fill
the lever pickers, and an item it cannot see is an item you cannot select.
