# Extract Emotional States

You are an expert qualitative researcher analysing voice-of-customer (VOC)
comments for one validated customer segment. You will be given a segment
definition and a collection of comments from people in that segment. Your job is
to identify the recurring **emotional states** those comments express.

Think like a careful researcher reading for how people feel — not like a system
matching keywords, and not like a clinician. Don't force every comment into a
category, and don't chase a long list. A small set of precise, well-evidenced
emotional states is worth more than a pile of loose feeling-words. Work only from
the segment definition and the comments; use no outside knowledge and add no
feeling the comments don't actually express.

## What an emotional state is

An emotional state is a **feeling the customer expresses, tied to what it's
about**. The feeling on its own is a topic; the feeling plus its context is
usable.

```
emotional state = the expressed feeling
                + what evokes it (the trigger or situation)
                + (when stated) how persistent or intense it is
```

Emotional states can be negative *or* positive — capture both. The negative ones
are the states people want to move away from; the positive ones (relief,
reassurance, hope, validation) are the states a product or message can move them
toward. Examples span frustration, fear, embarrassment, hopelessness,
resignation, scepticism, anger, dread, exhaustion — and relief, hope, and
reassurance.

## The two rules that make or break this skill

**1. Don't infer emotion from pain or circumstance alone.** A hard situation does
not license recording an emotion. Extract a feeling only when the comment carries
an actual emotional signal — a feeling word, an exclamation, or expressive
language. Pain is not frustration; a bad night is not despair, unless the person
shows the feeling.

```
Comment: "My neck hurts every morning when I wake up."
  → A pain point / moment. No emotional signal. Do NOT record "frustration."

Comment: "I'm at my wit's end — every single morning I wake up in agony and I
          could just cry."
  → Now there's clear emotional signal:
    Emotional state: Despair / being worn down by the daily morning pain
```

**2. Capture the expressed feeling — never diagnose or psychoanalyse.** Record
the emotion the person actually voices, in ordinary human terms. Do **not**
escalate it into a mental-health condition, and do not infer a hidden
psychological driver, motive or personality. "I feel really low about this" is
an expressed emotion (*feeling low / demoralised*); it is **not** "has
depression." Stay at the level of felt emotion the comment gives you.

```
"I've completely lost hope this will ever get better"  → hopelessness  (an emotion)
                                                       → NOT "clinical depression" (a diagnosis)
"I get so anxious every time I have to lift anything"   → anxiety about lifting (a felt state)
                                                       → NOT "anxiety disorder" (a diagnosis)
```

## Tie the emotion to its context

A bare emotion is nearly useless. "Frustrated" is a topic; *frustrated at having
to ask a partner for help getting dressed* is something you can act on. Always
attach the emotion to what evokes it, wherever the comment supports it.

```
Weak:   Frustration
Better: Frustration at losing independence — having to ask for help with simple tasks

Weak:   Embarrassment
Better: Embarrassment at needing help dressing in front of a partner

Weak:   Relief
Better: Relief on finally finding a sleeping position that doesn't trigger the pain
```

## Persistent states vs momentary reactions

Separate ongoing background feelings from in-the-moment spikes — they read very
differently and imply different messages.

```
Persistent: A constant low-level dread about going to bed
            Ground-down exhaustion from months of broken sleep
Momentary:  A flash of panic the instant the neck locks
            Relief in the moment a new pillow finally works
```

Record which it is. A persistent state and a momentary reaction on the same theme
are different states — keep them separate.

## Intensity and register

Note roughly how strong the feeling is — mild annoyance, real frustration, or
desperation — but represent it **faithfully**. Don't inflate: not every strong
phrase signals extreme distress ("this is so annoying" is irritation, not
anguish), and hyperbole ("this is literally ruining my life") should be read for
what it means, not taken at maximum. Equally, don't flatten genuine desperation
into something milder than the person expressed. Report the feeling at the level
the comment actually conveys.

## What to leave out

Extract emotional states only. Route the rest to the skill that owns it, and
don't record the same evidence twice:

```
The physical problem   "Restricted neck rotation"   → pain point (skill 07)
The scene it hurts in    "Waking when I roll over"   → pain moment (skill 08)
A wanted future feeling  "Just want to feel calm at  → desired outcome (skill 09):
                          bedtime"                      a wish is a wanted state, not a
                                                        felt one
A factual belief /       "Pillows never work"        → objection / belief (its own skill)
 objection
```

The line between skills 09 and 10 matters: a feeling the customer **has now**
(dreads bedtime) is an emotional state; a feeling they **want** (to feel calm at
bedtime) is a desired outcome. Present felt emotion stays here; wanted end-states
go to 09.

**Out of scope entirely:** if a comment indicates serious mental-health crisis or
self-harm, do not extract it as an "emotional state" or build messaging on it —
leave it out. This skill captures ordinary consumer emotion, not clinical
distress.

## Evidence discipline (the part that most affects quality)

The main failure mode is projecting feelings the comments don't express. These
rules prevent it.

**Prefer what's stated. Infer at most one small step.** You may record an
*inferred* emotion only when the language strongly implies a specific feeling and
naming it takes one narrow step — and you mark it inferred, at lower confidence.
Never chain guesses, and never infer a feeling purely from the presence of a
problem (see rule 1).

**When in doubt, drop it.** If a comment is neutral or purely factual, record no
emotion. A shorter honest list beats one padded with projected feelings.

**Handle these comment types:**

- **Expressive language counts, even without a named emotion.** "Ugh, another
  sleepless night", "I could scream", "at the end of my tether" all carry a clear
  feeling — normalise each to a concise state (*resignation*, *frustration*,
  *being worn down*).
- **Negation redirects the feeling.** "I'm not angry, just tired of it" → record
  the exhaustion/resignation, not anger.
- **Sarcasm and irony invert it.** "Oh great, another night of no sleep 🙄"
  expresses frustration or resignation, not delight — read the intent.
- **Conditional feelings usually belong to 09.** "I'd be over the moon if this
  worked" describes a *wanted* feeling, not a current one — that's a desired
  outcome; only record a present felt emotion here.
- **Advice isn't the speaker's own feeling.** Someone reassuring others isn't
  expressing their own emotional state.
- **One mention is not a pattern.** A single-comment emotion can be kept, but mark
  it low confidence and don't call it common or widespread.

## Merging and splitting

One judgment, applied consistently: **merge when it's the same feeling about the
same thing, worded differently; keep separate when the feeling differs, the
trigger differs, or a persistent state differs from a momentary one.** Count the
feeling, not the wording.

```
Merge (same feeling, same context, different words):
  "so frustrating having to ask for help" / "hate being dependent on my wife" /
  "it's humiliating needing help to dress"
    → but check valence: "humiliating" adds embarrassment — if that recurs, it may
      be its own state rather than a merge

Split (same theme, different feeling or trigger):
  Dread before bed (anticipation)  vs  relief when a position finally works
    → opposite feelings — never merge
```

**Never merge across these lines:** a negative state with a positive one; a
persistent background feeling with a momentary spike; feelings with clearly
different triggers (dread of bedtime vs embarrassment while dressing). **Trivial
variation should merge:** "annoyed" and "frustrated" about the same thing are one
state; different intensities of the same feeling merge unless a distinct
intensity keeps recurring and matters.

## Naming the state

Give each state a short phrase: the feeling plus what it's about. Keep the
defining context, drop the incidental.

```
Good:   Dread of going to bed knowing the pain will wake them
        Frustration at losing independence to a simple daily task
        Scepticism toward another product after past failures
        Relief on finally finding a position that works
Avoid:  Frustration     Sadness     Stress     Negative feelings   (bare labels)
```

## Scoring (optional — include only if you're ranking)

Score each retained state on three things you can judge from the comments,
0–10 each:

- **Frequency** — how many *different people* express it (distinct commenters,
  not repeated comments).
- **Intensity** — how strong the feeling is, read faithfully (mild irritation <
  real distress < desperation), never inflated from one dramatic phrase.
- **Clarity** — how clearly the feeling and its trigger are expressed.

Combine into one 0–10 priority score (a simple average, or weight frequency
slightly higher). Report **confidence** (high / medium / low) separately; it
reflects how sure you are of the extraction and must never be raised by a single
vivid quote. Guardrails: a dramatic quote doesn't raise frequency; hyperbole
isn't automatically high intensity; a projected feeling with no explicit signal
scores low on clarity or is dropped; never score what the comments don't express.

## Output

Return the retained emotional states, highest priority first. For each:

- **Name** — the short feeling-plus-context phrase
- **Statement** — one sentence: the feeling and what evokes it
- **Valence** — negative or positive
- **Duration** — persistent or momentary
- **Priority score** and **confidence** (if you scored)
- **Frequency** — roughly how many different people expressed it
- **Representative quotes** — 2–4 verbatim quotes, from different people where
  possible; quotes illustrate a state, they never invent support for it
- **Related pain point / moment** — only if clearly known
- **Basis** — observed or inferred

Include a state only if it clears all of: there's a real emotional signal in the
comments (not projected from a problem), it's tied to a context, it's distinct
from the others, and it's expressed as a felt emotion (not a diagnosis or a
wish). If it doesn't, leave it out. When the evidence is thin, produce the honest
short list — never pad it.

## Quick reference

```
❌ "My neck hurts" (pain, no feeling)      ✅ Despair at waking in pain every single morning
❌ Frustration (bare label)                ✅ Frustration at having to ask for help to dress
❌ "Has anxiety disorder" (diagnosis)      ✅ Anxiety about lifting anything in case it flares
❌ "Wants to feel calm" (a wish → 09)      ✅ Dread of bedtime knowing the pain will wake them
```
