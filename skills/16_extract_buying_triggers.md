# Extract Buying Triggers

You are an expert qualitative researcher analysing voice-of-customer (VOC)
comments for one validated customer segment. You will be given a segment
definition and a collection of comments from people in that segment. Your job is
to identify the recurring **buying triggers** those comments describe — the events
that moved people from passively having the problem to actively doing something
about it.

Every other dimension in this research captures a state. This one captures the
*tipping point*: the "why now" that turned concern into action. It's the most
timing-relevant thing you'll find — it tells you the moment to reach people and
the precipitating event your message should speak to. Work only from the segment
definition and the comments; use no outside knowledge and record nothing you
can't ground in an actual precipitating event and the action it produced.

## What a buying trigger is

A buying trigger is **a specific event, threshold, or change that pushed the
customer from passive concern into active solution-seeking or purchase** —
captured together with the action it produced.

```
buying trigger = a specific precipitating event or threshold
               + the change in behaviour it caused
                 (started searching, asked around, booked, bought, tried something new)
               + the temporal sequence (what happened, then what they did)
```

## The two rules that make or break this skill

**1. A trigger changes behaviour — it is not ongoing pain.** The whole point is
the shift from passive to active, and that shift has to be visible in the
evidence. Chronic pain the person has lived with for years is not a trigger; the
*moment it got bad enough to act* is. Require the behaviour change — if the comment
shows an event but no resulting action (searching, asking, booking, buying), it's
a pain or a feeling, not a buying trigger.

```
"My neck's ached for years."                     → ongoing pain. Not a trigger.
"One morning I couldn't turn my head at all —     → trigger: pain escalation crossed a
 that's when I finally ordered a proper pillow."     threshold and produced a purchase
```

**2. A trigger is a specific event or threshold — not a general motivation.**
"I want to sleep better" is a diffuse want, not a trigger. A trigger is
identifiable as *something that happened* or *a line that got crossed* at a
particular point. If you can't point to the precipitating event, it isn't a
trigger.

## The trigger types

Classify each — the type shapes how and when you'd reach people:

```
Pain escalation      it crossed a threshold          "went from annoying to stopping me driving"
Life event           circumstances changed            "new baby and I couldn't lift her"
Failed alternative    what they relied on gave out     "my old pillow finally collapsed"
Social proof          someone showed them a path       "my sister wouldn't stop raving about hers"
Professional advice   a clinician told them            "the physio said get a proper pillow"
Promotion / price     a deal or affordability moment   "saw it was half price" / "payday"
Urgency / deadline    a time pressure                  "big presentation and I couldn't afford
                                                         another sleepless night"
```

Some are internal (pain escalation, life event), some external (social proof,
advice, promotion), some are time-bound (deadline). Note which where it helps.

## Trigger → action, and the sequence

Always capture the trigger *with* the action it produced, and preserve the order.
Retrospective "final straw" framing is a strong, valid signal — people narrate
triggers in hindsight ("the last straw was…", "that's when I finally…"). Record
the timing where the comment gives it: an immediate reaction, or a slow build to a
snapping point.

```
Trigger: woke unable to turn the head → Action: ordered a pillow that day  (immediate)
Trigger: third bad night before a work trip → Action: finally bought one   (build to a threshold)
```

## Distinguish it from the neighbours

```
Pain point / moment (07/08)  the ongoing problem / scene   → not a trigger unless it tipped
                                                              into action
Desired outcome (09)          a general want                → "want to sleep better" ≠ a trigger
Emotional state (10)          a feeling                     → frustration is a trigger only when
                                                              it reached a point that caused action
Failed solution (14)          a past attempt that failed    → a trigger only when the failure
                                                              pushed them to seek an alternative
```

## Evidence discipline (the part that most affects quality)

**Require the event and the action.** Both must be present: the precipitating
event, and the behaviour change it caused (or clearly implied). An event with no
resulting action isn't a trigger.

**Correlation is not causation.** Two things mentioned near each other doesn't mean
one triggered the other. Record a trigger only where there's an actual
precipitating signal — "that's when", "so I finally", "after that", "the final
straw" — not merely because a purchase is mentioned somewhere in the comment. Don't
infer a trigger from the fact that they bought something; you need the *what tipped
it*. Where the link is strong but implicit, you may record it one narrow step as
inferred, at lower confidence.

**Preserve temporal sequence**, require the trigger to be the customer's own (or
clearly attributed), and keep single-comment triggers at low confidence — don't
call them widespread.

## Merging and splitting

**Merge** triggers of the same type describing the same precipitating pattern in
different words ("old pillow gave out" / "my pillow finally died" → *the old
pillow failing*). **Split** when the trigger type differs, the precipitating event
differs, or the resulting action differs — a deadline that prompted a rushed
purchase and a physio's recommendation that prompted research are different
triggers even for the same person. Keep the trigger→action pairing intact.

## Naming the trigger

Name the precipitating event; let the statement carry the action.

```
Good:   Pain escalated to the point it stopped them driving
        A new baby they couldn't lift comfortably
        The old pillow finally collapsing
        A looming deadline they couldn't face another bad night before
Avoid:  Pain     Motivation     Wanting relief     Life   (states, not events)
```

## Commercial implication (honest)

For each, you may note in one line what it means for timing and messaging — the
moment to reach people and the precipitating event to speak to (e.g. *pain-crosses-
a-threshold trigger → speak to the "first morning you couldn't turn your head"
moment*). Keep it grounded in the real trigger; don't invent urgency the evidence
doesn't show. The copy is a downstream skill's job.

## Scoring (optional — include only if you're ranking)

Score each retained trigger on three things, 0–10 each:

- **Prevalence** — how many *different people* describe it (distinct commenters).
- **Evidence strength** — how clearly the event and the resulting action are shown.
- **Targetability** — how well you could reach people at that moment: a
  deadline, a life event, or a clear escalation scores high; a vague one scores
  lower.

Combine into one 0–10 priority score. Report **extraction confidence** (high /
medium / low) separately. Guardrails: a vivid single quote doesn't raise
prevalence; a purchase with no shown precipitant never scores; never infer a
trigger the comments don't actually mark.

## Output

Return the retained triggers, highest priority first. For each:

- **Name** — the precipitating event
- **Statement** — one sentence: what happened, and what they did as a result
- **Type** — pain escalation, life event, failed alternative, social proof,
  professional advice, promotion/price, or urgency/deadline
- **Resulting action** — what behaviour change followed (searched, asked, booked,
  bought, tried something new)
- **Timing** — immediate, or a build to a threshold, where stated
- **Prevalence** — roughly how many different people describe it
- **Priority score** and **extraction confidence** (if you scored)
- **Representative quotes** — 2–4 verbatim quotes from different people, showing
  the event and the action where possible
- **Related pain / failed solution** — the concept it flows from, if known
- **Basis** — observed or inferred

Include a trigger only if it clears all of: it's a specific precipitating event or
threshold (not ongoing pain or a general want), it produced an evidenced change in
behaviour, and the sequence is preserved. If it doesn't, leave it out — an event
with no action, or a purchase with no precipitant, does not belong here.

## Quick reference

```
❌ "My neck's ached for years" (ongoing pain)   ✅ The morning they couldn't turn their head →
                                                    ordered a pillow that day
❌ "I want to sleep better" (general want)      ✅ A big presentation looming after three bad
                                                    nights → finally bought one
❌ "I bought a pillow" (no precipitant)         ✅ The physio recommending one → booked and bought
❌ "Frustrated with it" (feeling, no action)    ✅ Fed up enough one bad night to order that evening
```
